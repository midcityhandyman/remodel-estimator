# Assumptions and where they come from

This is the long form of the table in the README. Every default in `data/catalog.json` that drives the bathroom, kitchen and basement examples was looked up on 2026-11-24 and given one of three labels:

- **confirmed**: a source that was opened and read says the same thing, or gives a range that the value sits inside.
- **corrected**: a source disagreed, so the catalog was changed. The old and new values are both given.
- **judgment**: no authoritative source was found, or the sources that matter could not be opened. The value stays, and it is a call another contractor could make differently.

All prices and rates in this repository are samples a contractor should replace with their own. A confirmed label means the default is defensible, not that it fits your crew, your products or your site.

## How to read the sources

Product documents are described by type and not by brand, because this repository names no suppliers. Documents from standards bodies and handbooks are named by their public titles. No page numbers or clause numbers are given for anything that was not read directly.

Could not open, and so not relied on:

- The tile industry's installation handbook (Handbook for Ceramic, Glass, and Stone Tile Installation). It is a paid publication. Nothing here is attributed to it. Tile board, mortar, membrane and grout items rest on manufacturer documents instead.
- GA-214, Levels of Finish for Gypsum Panel Products, and GA-216, Application and Finishing of Gypsum Panel Products (Gypsum Association). The links to the association's copies failed. Their content is taken second hand, from documents that restate them, and that is said on each item.
- The official text of the International Residential Code. The publisher's site refused the request. The decay protection rule is taken from a trade journal's code Q&A and from a code training site, both of which quote it.
- Published labor guides (annual cost data books and trade association labor unit manuals). They are paid publications. No hours per unit figure in the catalog could be checked against one.

Sources that were opened and read:

1. GA-236-2023, Joint Treatment Under Extreme Weather Conditions (Gypsum Association): the drying time table for premixed joint compound by temperature and humidity.
2. The Gypsum Construction Handbook, chapter 3, Cladding (a gypsum board maker's handbook, Canadian edition): order of application and the fastener spacing table.
3. A gypsum board maker's sheet, Drywall Materials Estimator and Coverage: compound, tape and screws by wall area.
4. A premixed all purpose joint compound product data submittal sheet: coverage by weight, coat sequence, temperature.
5. A trade magazine's drywall project guide article on joint compound: tape and compound per 1,000 sf, drying.
6. A gypsum board maker's published summary of the levels of finish in GA-214.
7. A cement board installation guide from the board's maker: floors, walls and ceilings.
8. A fiber cement backer board maker's floor installation page.
9. A liquid waterproofing and crack isolation membrane submittal sheet.
10. A second maker's liquid waterproofing membrane product data sheet.
11. A third maker's membrane family sell sheet: coverage, and a comparison of cure times before flood testing. It is a sales sheet for a faster product, so its cure times for ordinary membranes are weak evidence.
12. A sanded grout technical data sheet: mortar cure before grouting, time before foot traffic.
13. A second maker's sanded grout product data sheet: time before traffic and shower use, grout coverage by tile and joint size.
14. A tile mortar maker's coverage charts (product page and family sell sheet): square feet per 50 lb bag by trowel notch.
15. A shower base maker's installation manual.
16. A rigid vinyl plank maker's installation instructions.
17. An interior latex paint technical data sheet.
18. A drywall primer maker's product page.
19. A trade journal code Q&A, Is Treated Wood Always Required for Sills?, and a code training site's summary of the 2018 International Residential Code section on protection of wood against decay.
20. Two evaluation reports from a code evaluation service for powder driven pins used to fasten wood sill plates to concrete.

## Tile substrate and shower

**Floor tile underlayment** (confirmed). 1/4 inch cement backer board over a wood subfloor. The cement board guide (7) lists 1/4, 1/2 and 5/8 inch board for floors over a subfloor of at least 5/8 inch plywood or OSB, and the fiber cement page (8) calls for 1/4 inch board on floors. Site conditions: both set limits on the subfloor and joist spacing (16 inches on center, 24 at most in source 8). An uncoupling membrane is a different system and is not in the catalog.

**Thinset bed under floor backer board** (confirmed). Both board makers (7, 8) require a bed of latex modified mortar combed with a 1/4 inch square notch trowel, with the board fastened while the mortar is wet. The catalog buys one 50 lb bag per 75 sf for the bed and the seam fill. Mortar coverage charts (14) give 85 to 100 sf per bag at that notch and a search listing for another product gave 77, so 75 is just under the low end, which leaves room for the seams.

**Backer board fastener spacing** (confirmed). 8 inches on center on floors, around the perimeter and in the field, and 8 inches on center on walls into studs at 16 inches, with 1-1/4 inch backer screws (7). Ceilings are 6 inches on center (7). The fiber cement page (8) says the same 8 inches over the whole floor sheet, 3/8 to 3/4 inch from edges and 2 inches from corners.

**Backer board screws per square foot** (judgment). No source gives a count. It is worked out from the spacing: a 3 x 5 ft sheet on a floor takes 40 to 54 screws depending on how the edges are handled, which is 2.7 to 3.6 per sf. The catalog uses 3.7 (one box of 185 per 50 sf), the high end. Walls work out to 1.7 to 2.0 per sf and the catalog uses about 2 (one box per 90 sf).

**Backer board screw box size** (confirmed). 1-1/4 inch cement board screws are sold in boxes of 185 (22). This is weak evidence from retail listings and it only matters for the box arithmetic. Check the count on the box you buy.

**Floor backer board after paint and wall board** (judgment). The catalog hangs the wall board early, paints, then puts down the floor board right before the floor tile, to keep the new floor out of the mess. Neither board maker (7, 8) sets an order. They only require a gap between the floor board and the walls (1/8 or 1/4 inch). Many tile setters board the floor earlier. Change the phase if you do.

**Shower wall board** (confirmed). 1/2 inch cement board on studs at no more than 16 inches on center, seams covered with alkali resistant mesh tape set in mortar (7). The guide allows only 1/2 and 5/8 inch board on walls.

**Shower pan before backer board** (confirmed). The shower base manual (15) sets the base against the bare studs, marks the top of its tile flange on them, beds the base in mortar, and only then has the cement board installed above the flange. One manual was read. A site built mortar pan is a different sequence and is not in the catalog.

**Liquid membrane coverage** (confirmed). Two coats at about 50 sf per gallon for the finished membrane. The three membrane documents give 40 to 50 sf per gallon for two coats on smooth board with less on rough board such as cement board (9), 50 sf per gallon (10) and 55 sf per gallon (11). The range is 40 to 55 and the catalog is in the middle, then adds 5 percent waste. On rough board expect the low end.

**Membrane cure before tile** (confirmed). One day. This is the long end. A coat dries in half an hour to an hour and a half at 70 F and 50 percent humidity (9). Full cure before a flood test is 2 hours at 70 F or warmer for one product and 24 hours between 50 and 69 F (10), and about 12 to 24 hours for another (9). The sales sheet (11) puts ordinary membranes at a day in warm dry rooms and several days in cold wet ones. Site conditions: below 50 F these products are not to be used at all (9), and a cold damp room needs more than the one day.

**Shower tiled while the drywall coats dry** (judgment). Waterproofing and wall tile wait for the boards to be hung, not for the drywall finishing. No source requires or forbids it. Sanding dust on fresh tile is the cost.

## Drywall

**Drywall coat schedule** (confirmed). Hang, tape coat, second coat, finish coat, sand. A board maker's summary of GA-214 (6) describes Level 4 as tape embedded in the joints and angles, two coats over the flat joints, one over the angles and three over the fasteners, which is the tape coat plus two more passes. The compound data sheet (4) describes the same embed, second and finish coats and lists GA-214 and GA-216 as its finishing guidelines. GA-214 itself could not be opened.

**Drywall dry time per coat** (confirmed). One dry day after each coat of premixed all purpose compound. GA-236-2023 (1) gives the drying time of one coat about 1/16 inch thick: 17 hours at 70 F and 50 percent humidity, 24 hours at 60 F and 50 percent, 26 hours at 70 F and 70 percent, 38 hours at 60 F and 70 percent, 36 hours at 50 F and 50 percent, and three days at 70 F and 90 percent. So one day holds in a heated, ventilated room at about 60 to 70 F and 50 percent humidity or drier. It does not hold in a cool damp basement or a bathroom with no ventilation, where two days a coat is the honest figure. The same document and the compound data sheet (4) call for at least 50 F from 48 hours before until the compound is dry. The table assumes adequate ventilation, and thicker coats take longer in proportion.

**Joint compound quantity** (confirmed). One 4.5 gallon bucket per 450 sf across the three coats, which is 10 gallons per 1,000 sf. The estimator sheet (3) gives 9.0 gallons (123 to 140 lb), the trade article (5) about 11 gallons for a three coat job, and the data sheet (4) 100 to 150 lb. The range is 9 to 11 gallons and the catalog is in the middle.

**Joint tape quantity** (corrected). Was one 250 ft roll per 600 sf (417 ft per 1,000 sf). Sources give 350 ft (3) and 370 ft (5) per 1,000 sf. Now one roll per 675 sf, which is 370 ft per 1,000 sf, the higher of the two. A small room with many inside corners can run over.

**Drywall screws** (corrected). Was one 5 lb box per 900 sf of wall or 750 sf of ceiling, with a claim of about 1,300 screws in a box. The estimator sheet (3) gives 1,250 screws per 1,000 sf with no split between walls and ceilings. Retail listings (22) put a 5 lb box of 1-1/4 inch screws at about 1,190 to 1,355. Now one box per 1,000 sf for both.

**Drywall screw spacing** (confirmed). 12 inches on center on ceilings and 16 on walls for a single layer on wood framing at 16 inches (2). The same table gives 12 inches on walls for water resistant board. GA-216 could not be opened.

**Ceilings before walls** (confirmed). The handbook (2) says to apply ceiling boards first and to hang the walls after the ceilings so the wall board supports the ceiling edges. GA-216, which a search listing quotes to the same effect, could not be opened.

## Tile

**Tile set and grout as separate visits** (corrected). Was one visit for setting and grouting with one cure day after it. A grout data sheet (12) requires the setting mortar to cure 24 to 48 hours before cement grout goes on, unless the mortar is rapid setting. Grout must then be left alone: no foot traffic for 16 hours (12), or no walking or disturbing for 24 hours, no heavy traffic for 72 hours and no shower use for 24 to 48 hours (13). Now each tile item is two visits: set, one cure day, grout, one more day. This adds two working days to each example and changes no hours and no materials. How the hours divide between setting and grouting (about four fifths and one fifth) is a judgment. The 24 hours is the short end of the mortar cure, and both sheets want 50 F or warmer for 72 hours.

**Thinset and grout quantities** (judgment). One 50 lb bag of thinset per 50 sf of tile and one 25 lb bag of grout per 100 sf. Both sit inside published ranges, but the ranges are wide because they depend on the tile. Mortar charts (14) give 60 to 67 sf per bag with a 1/4 x 3/8 inch notch and 42 to 47 with a 1/2 inch notch. A grout chart (13) gives about 80 to 160 sf per bag for 12 inch tile and 58 to 106 for 2 inch mosaic, by joint width. Use the chart for your tile.

**Tile waste by layout** (judgment). 10 percent on floors and backsplashes, 12 percent on shower walls, and a per item override for other layouts. No standards body figure was found. A tile seller's guide (21) recommends 10 percent as the baseline and 15 percent or more for diagonal and other patterned layouts, and the plank maker (16) likewise says 10 percent and more for diagonal. The 12 percent on shower walls is this catalog's own allowance for niches and cuts at the pan. For a diagonal or herringbone layout set the item's `waste` to 0.15 or more.

**Tile on a slab** (judgment). No backer board: the slab is scraped, patched and tiled. Moisture testing and crack isolation are left out, which is a real gap on an old basement slab.

## Flooring and paint

**Floating plank after base cabinets** (confirmed). The plank maker's instructions (16) say permanent cabinets, vanities and islands go in first and the floating floor is laid around them with the expansion gap, and that permanently mounted cabinets are not to sit on the floor. Search listings for three other makers' instructions say the same. The kitchen example takes the cabinet footprint out of the floor quantity.

**Floating plank waste** (corrected). Was 8 percent. The instructions (16) say to order 10 percent extra for cuts, and more for a diagonal layout. Now 10 percent. The same instructions want a 1/4 inch gap at every wall and fixed object, and over a slab a 6 mil plastic sheet where moisture is a risk. The catalog's underlayment roll does not stand in for that sheet.

**Primer and paint coverage and coats** (confirmed). One coat of drywall primer at 300 sf per gallon and two finish coats at 350 sf per gallon. The paint data sheet (17) gives 300 to 400 sf per gallon depending on texture and porosity, and for new drywall one coat of drywall primer and two coats of paint. The primer page (18) gives 300 to 400 sf per gallon. The catalog uses the low end for primer and the middle for paint. Paint is one visit: the 2 hour recoat time (17) fits inside it.

## Framing on a slab

**Pressure treated bottom plate on a slab** (confirmed). The residential code's decay protection rule lists sills and sleepers on a concrete slab that is in direct contact with the ground, unless an impervious moisture barrier separates them (19). The rule as quoted does not single out interior walls. The official code text could not be opened, and your jurisdiction's edition and amendments govern.

**Sill sealer under the plate** (judgment). The journal Q&A (19) describes sill seal as an air seal and a capillary break. No source was found that requires it under an interior partition. It is cheap, and it stays in the default.

**Slab fastener spacing** (confirmed). About every 32 inches, with a pin 6 inches from each plate end. The two evaluation reports (20) allow at most 3 ft and at most 4 ft between pins for interior walls that are not bearing or bracing walls, up to 14 ft tall, and one of them requires the pin 6 inches from the plate ends. The takeoff buys a box of 100 per 250 lf of wall, which is one every 30 inches. Those reports cover specific pins. Use the spacing in the report for the fastener you buy, and check that it is rated for treated lumber, which was not verified here.

**Double top plate** (judgment). A habit carried over from bearing walls. No source was checked.

## Sequence and labor

**Electrical rough-in and trim-out split** (judgment). Two visits, with about two thirds of the device labor at rough-in (0.6 hours rough, 0.3 hours finish, plus half an hour of setup on the trim-out visit). The order is ordinary practice. The split is not from a published labor unit manual: none could be opened.

**Rough plumbing hours per fixture** (judgment). 4 hours per fixture plus 1 hour of setup, meant as an average over a toilet, a lavatory and a tub or shower. An earlier note in the catalog quoted a published range for this. That range could not be verified and has been removed.

**Shower valve in two visits** (judgment). Valve body at rough-in (2 hours), trim and shower head after the tile is grouted (0.75 hours).

**Baseboard deductions** (judgment). Perimeter less 3 lf per door opening, less the runs listed in `base_deductions`. An estimating convention. The estimate prints the arithmetic so it can be checked.

**Trim painted after it is installed** (judgment). Primed trim goes in after wall paint and gets its own visit for filling, caulk and two coats.

**Visits rounded to half days** (judgment). A scheduling simplification, not a trade rule.

**Production rates** (judgment). Every hours per unit and setup figure in the catalog. The README used to say these were checked against published labor guides. That could not be verified, because the guides are paid publications that were not opened, so the claim has been withdrawn. Treat every rate as a placeholder for your own job records. See "Compared against the author's own estimates" below for how they line up with planned hours on real estimates.

**Bathroom gut total labor hours** (judgment). The 5 x 8 ft bathroom example comes to about 104 labor hours across all trades. No authoritative published figure for a job of this size was found. It is the sum of the judgment rates above, so it is no better than they are. Two of the author's own bathroom estimates plan 92 and 138.5 hours per bathroom, so 104 sits inside that range.

## Compared against the author's own estimates

Added 2026-11-24. The author runs a remodeling company. Its job records were searched for hours to test the sample rates against. No tracked time entries or daily logs were found, so there are no actual hours here. What was found is planned labor hours on a few past estimates. The table sets those beside what this engine produces for the same quantity.

| Line | Engine, sample rates | Author's estimates, planned hours | Estimates | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Small bathroom gut, all trades | 104.0 hours (bathroom example) | 92 and 138.5 hours per bathroom | 2 | inside the range |
| Bathroom demolition | 13.8 hours | 14 and 20 hours per bathroom | 2 | low end of the range |
| Tile set and grout, 40 sf floor plus 75 sf shower wall | 24.9 hours | 24 hours | 1 | close |
| Plumbing rough-in plus setting fixtures, one bathroom | 21.5 hours, plus 2.0 for the vanity | about 20 and 29 hours | 2 | inside the range |
| Electrical with exhaust fan, one bathroom | 8.7 hours | about 8 and 14 hours | 2 | low end of the range |
| Floating plank floor, 164 sf | 9.2 hours | 8 hours | 1 | close |
| Base and wall cabinets, per linear foot | 0.53 hours (kitchen example, 26 lf) | 0.55 hours (22 lf) | 1 | close |
| Baseboard, 220 lf | 11.5 hours | 16 hours | 1 | engine is about 30 percent low |

What this does and does not show:

- These are planned hours, written before the work. Nothing here says the jobs were built in those hours.
- Each line rests on one or two estimates. That is enough to say a rate is not wildly off, and not enough to set it.
- The larger bathroom estimate includes framing (about 9 percent of its hours) and a setup and cleanup allowance (about 6.5 percent). The bathroom example here has no framing item, which accounts for part of the gap between 104 and 138.5.
- Baseboard is the one line where the records sit clearly above the sample rate. One estimate plans about 7.3 hours per 100 lf against 5 hours per 100 lf plus half an hour of setup here. If your trim runs like that estimate, set `hours_per_unit` for `baseboard` to 0.07.
- No records were found for: hours in a working day, crew sizes, a pull and replace bathroom, a basement finish of this scope, drywall hang and finish separately, paint on its own, doors, occupied against empty houses, overruns, or trade minimums.

So the rates keep the status judgment. The comparison moves them from unchecked to roughly consistent with one contractor's planning numbers.

## What changed in the catalog

| Item | Old | New | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tile floor, shower wall tile, backsplash | one visit, then one cure day | set, one cure day, grout, one more day | grout data sheets require 24 to 48 hours of mortar cure before grouting |
| Paper joint tape | one 250 ft roll per 600 sf | one roll per 675 sf | sources give 350 to 370 ft per 1,000 sf |
| Drywall screws | one 5 lb box per 900 sf of wall, 750 sf of ceiling | one box per 1,000 sf | estimator sheet gives 1,250 screws per 1,000 sf |
| Floating plank waste | 8 percent | 10 percent | plank maker's instructions |
| Provenance of the labor rates | described as checked against published guides | described as judgment | the guides could not be opened |

The sample totals did not move: every corrected quantity still rounds up to the same whole boxes, rolls and cartons in the three examples. The schedules did: each example is two working days longer.
